Independent Samples: Building the Pooled t Statistic

We build the test statistic together, step by step. Then you finish the test yourself, exactly as in Week 11.

The business question

A company runs two customer-service centers. It timed a random sample of 12 calls at Center A and 10 different calls at Center B (minutes). At α = 0.05, is there a difference in the mean call-handling time between the two centers?

Paired or independent?

What exactly are we testing?

Samples
12 calls at A, 10 different calls at B
Populations
All calls at A; all calls at B
Parameter
μ1 − μ2

Group 1 = Center A, group 2 = Center B. We estimate μ1 − μ2 with x̄1 − x̄2.

Null hypothesisH₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = 0

On average, the two centers take the same time.

Alternative hypothesisH₁: μ₁ − μ₂ ≠ 0

The mean times differ.

Two-tailed, because the question asks whether the times are different, not whether one is longer.

Describe each group

We do not know σ1 or σ2, so we estimate the spread of each population with its sample standard deviation, just as in Week 11.

The sample means differ by x̄₁ − x̄₂ = 0.915 minutes. Is that gap bigger than what random sampling alone would produce? To answer, we need a standard error.

Building the test statistic: what goes inside?

We are now ready to compute our test statistic, t calc. You can already guess its shape from Weeks 9–11:

t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂ − 0) ÷ ( s ∕ √n )
(estimate − value under H₀) ÷ standard error

But now we have two groups. So what do we put for s, and what do we put for n? Click each card.

For s: we have two, s₁ = 0.769 and s₂ = 0.600. If the two spreads are similar, we combine them into one pooled value, sp. The next step checks whether pooling is OK; the step after that computes sp.
For n: we have two, n₁ = 12 and n₂ = 10. Each group adds its own sampling uncertainty, so the single 1/n becomes 1/n₁ + 1/n₂.
Putting it together
t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂ − 0) ÷ √[ sp² (1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ]
Same idea as before: s ∕ √n = √(s² × 1/n). We just swap in sp² and 1/n₁ + 1/n₂.

Can we pool? A quick equal-variance check

The pooled t test combines s₁ and s₂ into one estimate of the common spread. That only makes sense if the two populations have similar spread (σ₁ = σ₂).

larger s ÷ smaller s = 0.769 ÷ 0.600 = 1.28
Rule of thumb: a ratio below 2 means pooling is reasonable
1.28 < 2 → pooling is reasonable. We use the pooled (equal-variance) t test.

If the ratio were 2 or more, we would still run the pooled test in this course but add a caution sentence. (The Welch test, T.TEST type 3, does not assume equal variances. It is not covered.)

The pooled variance sp²

A weighted average of the two sample variances, with weights n − 1:

sp² = [ (n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂² ] ÷ (n₁ + n₂ − 2)
sp² = [ (12 − 1)(0.769)² + (10 − 1)(0.600)² ] ÷ (12 + 10 − 2)
    = [ 11(0.591) + 9(0.360) ] ÷ 20 = (6.503 + 3.244) ÷ 20 = 0.487
0.487 sits between s₂² = 0.360 and s₁² = 0.591, closer to Center A's value because Center A has more calls.

Excel: =((n1-1)*s1^2 + (n2-1)*s2^2)/(n1+n2-2)

Standard error, t, and df

SE = √[ sp² (1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ]   ·   t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂ − 0) ÷ SE   ·   df = n₁ + n₂ − 2
SE = √[ 0.487 × (1/12 + 1/10) ] = √(0.487 × 0.183) = 0.299
t = (7.175 − 6.260 − 0) ÷ 0.299 = 0.915 ÷ 0.299 = 3.061
df = 12 + 10 − 2 = 20
The observed gap measured in standard errors
The gap between the two sample means is about 3.06 standard errors away from 0, the value H₀ claims.
